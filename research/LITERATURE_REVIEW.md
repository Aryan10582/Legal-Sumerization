# Analytical literature comparison

Numbers refer to the final twenty-entry journal bibliography. Summaries use original publisher abstracts/pages and available author work; some complete subscription texts were unavailable. Findings below are literature findings, not scores reproduced here. Limits and project implications are our assessment. Dataset details not exposed by an accessible abstract are marked rather than guessed.

| Ref. | Task / data | Method and reported finding | Limit and implication for this project |
|---|---|---|---|
| [1] Kanapala et al. | Survey of legal-document summarization | Organizes approaches and domain-specific obstacles rather than contributing a new comparable benchmark score. | Predates current generators; motivates separating extraction, generation and evaluation. Retained as a foundational legal survey. |
| [2] Sharma et al. | Comparative Indian legal summarization study | Compares summarization techniques for Indian documents; demonstrates that model choice and evaluation setup matter. | Small evaluation setting cannot establish universal superiority; motivates a documented within-dataset baseline comparison. |
| [3] Chalkidis and Kampas | Legal representations for several NLP tasks | Legal-corpus word embeddings and deep-learning adaptation illustrate benefits of domain text. | Domain adaptation is not Indian jurisdiction validation or summary generation; motivates legal encoder classification with explicit transfer limits. |
| [4] Saravanan and Ravindran | Rhetorical segmentation of expert-annotated judgments | Conditional random fields identify rhetorical roles for structured summarization. | Requires role annotations absent from our selected paired corpus; supports role-aware selection as future work, not a completed feature. |
| [5] Koniaris et al. | Greek case-law summarization | Develops/evaluates legal text summarization on Greek case-law material and compares techniques. | Greek language, legal structure and summaries differ from IN-Abs; motivates jurisdiction-specific test claims. |
| [6] Huang et al. | Legal judgment summaries, CAIL2020 and LCRD | Two-stage selection and keyword-guided generation report gains over their baselines. | A selector can irreversibly omit information; motivates our transparent select-then-generate pipeline and coverage traces. |
| [7] Anand and Wagh | Indian legal text summarization | Neural models and embeddings use summary-derived sentence supervision, reporting effective legal extraction. | Automatic sentence labels approximate reference relevance; motivates acknowledging the weak labels in our separate CUAD conversion. |
| [8] Feijo and Moreira | Abstractive legal ruling summaries | LegalSumm combines generation with textual-entailment-based selection; reports improved summaries. | Entailment scores do not prove legal correctness; motivates manual inspection of disposition and attributed statements alongside ROUGE. |
| [9] Jain et al. | BillSum and FIRE legal summarization | DCESumm combines supervised sentence relevance with unsupervised clustering-based score enhancement; reports ROUGE gains. | Results are dataset-specific and do not transfer automatically to short IN-Abs summaries; suggests better content diversity than simple centrality. |
| [10] Deroy et al. | Extractive legal case judgments including Indian corpora | Ensembles sentence-ranking methods and reports improvements in overlap metrics. | Ensemble complexity and overlap metrics leave factual completeness unresolved; motivates an inexpensive, reproducible extractive comparator. |
| [11] Deroy et al. | Indian/UK judgments and US government reports | Compares large/generative models for legal summarization and identifies useful overlap performance. | Generated text can contain unsupported information; motivates showing source and output together and avoiding an automatic legal-answer claim. |
| [12] Dan et al. | Structurally segmented legal judgment summaries | Splits judgments into three parts; combines Lawformer-based extractive and abstractive models; reports gains over LEAD-3. | Structural assumptions and long-encoder compute differ from our setting; motivates preserving legal roles in future selectors. Accessible abstract does not establish Indian evaluation. |
| [13] Y. Gao et al. | Judicial summarization, CAIL2020 | Hybrid extraction/generation uses legal domain knowledge and contrastive objectives; reports improved benchmark performance. | Domain knowledge and document conventions require adaptation; motivates checking extracted legal issues rather than borrowing literature scores. |
| [14] W. Gao et al. | Chinese CAIL2020 legal summaries | LSDK-LegalSum uses logical parts and domain knowledge in sequential summarization; reports ROUGE improvements. | Chinese structural labels are not supplied in IN-Abs; motivates a possible separately annotated Indian rhetorical-role extension. |
| [15] Moon et al. | Construction specifications, 56 documents / 2,807 clauses in the study | Fine-tuned BERT detects contractual risk categories and reports effective classification. | Risk taxonomy differs from CUAD clause types and is not Indian; motivates a transformer comparator while refusing a risk/enforceability interpretation. |
| [16] Zhang et al. | International construction contract clauses; small original document pool | GPT-driven augmentation supports multi-label classification, reporting improved classification/error metrics. | Synthetic expansions can leak source-specific wording and need audit; motivates document-first splits, with augmentation left unimplemented. |
| [17] Dadas et al. | Polish B2C contracts and abusive-clause material | Transformer detection plus retrieval supports human contract review. | Abuse labels and consumer law differ from neutral CUAD clause categories; motivates evidence-linked review rather than universal legal advice. |

| [20] Munthuli et al. | Thai Supreme Court decision fragments and multiple criminal intents | Fine-tuned transformers are compared with an embedding/SVM approach for multi-intent classification. | Thai criminal intents are not CUAD categories; overlapping intent errors motivate sigmoid outputs and per-label analysis, without importing its scores. |

## Defensible gap

The reviewed literature already includes legal summarizers, hybrid selection/generation and transformer clause classifiers. This project makes no first-system claim. Its contribution is a small reproducible teaching implementation that keeps Indian judgment summarization and non-Indian clause labeling separate while exposing evidence, content-selection limits, weak label conversion and actual baseline comparisons in one local interface. The empirical gap remaining after the pilot is independent Indian contract annotation/evaluation and legal faithfulness assessment under longer summary budgets.
